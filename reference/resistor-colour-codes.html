0 · The short answer for your box

Pick this one

Use the 1 kΩ resistor — brown · black · red · gold — with a red LED.

Using a typical 2.0 V red LED, the simple estimate is (3.3 − 2.0) ÷ 1000 = 1.3 mA. That is gentle on the Pico and normally visible indoors.

Your 100 Ω resistor is brown · black · brown · gold. The same estimate gives 13 mA. That exceeds the RP2350 pad’s default 4 mA drive setting, so it is not our beginner default even though it appears inside the tutorial’s broad 50–330 Ω range.

Start with red, not white White and blue LEDs have a forward voltage close to the Pico’s 3.3 V output, leaving little predictable voltage across the resistor. They may be faint and make good wiring look broken. Do not “fix” that by removing the resistor or moving the circuit to 5 V. Start with red.

1 · How to spot which resistor is which

A resistor has no numbers printed on it. It has 4 or 5 coloured stripes, and those stripes are the number. Reading them takes three steps: find the starting end, translate each colour to a digit, then apply the multiplier.

Step 1 — find which end to start from

Read it from the wrong end and 220 Ω becomes 1.2 Ω. Use these tests, in order:

  1. Look for gold or silver. That is the tolerance band and it is always last. Put it on the right, read leftwards-to-rightwards from the other end.
  2. Look at the gaps. The value bands are grouped close together; there is a visibly wider gap before the final tolerance band. Wide gap goes on the right.
  3. Look at which end the group sits nearer. The first band is usually closer to its lead than the last band is to its own.
  4. Read it both ways and pick the sensible one. Resistors only come in standard values. Only one direction will land on a real value — the other gives something absurd like 1.2 Ω or 91 GΩ.

Step 2 — translate the colours

One table does everything. The digit above each swatch is what the colour means in a digit position; the value below is what the same colour means in the multiplier position.

Colour coding

    The two half-multipliers, gold (×0.1) and silver (×0.01), only ever appear in the multiplier position — that is how sub-10 Ω values are written.

    Cross-check: Vishay’s manufacturer colour-code card.

    Step 3 — know the two layouts

    • 4 bands — digit, digit, multiplier, tolerance. Usually a beige/tan body, usually a gold ±5% band. This is what a starter kit is full of.
    • 5 bands — digit, digit, digit, multiplier, tolerance. Usually a pale blue body and a brown ±1% band. Precision parts.

    Body colour can be a clue, not a rule. Count the bands and identify the separated tolerance band.

    The three confusions that actually bite

    Brown vs red vs orange. Under warm indoor light these look nearly identical, and they are digits 1, 2 and 3 — an easy 10× error. Hold the resistor against white paper in daylight, or beside a resistor you have already identified.

    Gold vs yellow. Gold is metallic and shiny, yellow is flat. If it is the last band with a gap before it, it is gold.

    Red vs orange when faded. Older or heat-stressed resistors fade. When it matters, measure it.

    The check that ends every argument A multimeter set to Ω, one probe on each leg, resistor out of the circuit. That is the ground truth — colour bands are a label, not a measurement. Anything within the tolerance band (±5% on a gold-band part) is a healthy resistor.

    Two more things the body tells you

    • Size means power rating, not value. A ¼ W resistor has a body roughly 6.5 × 2.3 mm. Bigger body, more watts it can dissipate. Nothing in this project gets near ¼ W, so any size is fine.
    • A single black band and nothing else is a 0 Ω link — a wire in resistor clothing. Not a resistor you want on an LED.
    • Direction does not matter. A resistor is symmetrical; you can fit it either way round. Only the LED cares about direction.

      3 · How to choose an LED resistor

      Why any resistor at all

      An LED is a diode, not a resistor. Its current rises sharply after reaching its forward voltage, Vf. It cannot safely set its own current.

      Connect one straight from a 3.3 V pin to ground and the GPIO pin plus the LED’s nonlinear behaviour determine the current. That can overload either part.

      The resistor is the part that does obey Ohm's law. Putting one in series makes the resistor — not the LED — decide the current. That is its whole job here, which is why it is called a current-limiting resistor.

      Where the numbers come from

      The LED drops its Vf no matter what. Everything left over lands across the resistor. So:

      Vresistor = Vsupply − Vf
      R = ( Vsupply − Vf ) ÷ Itarget

      For an estimate: the Pico 2 GPIO uses 3.3 V. A red LED often has Vf around 2.0 V, but the exact value depends on its datasheet, current, and temperature. That leaves about 1.3 V across the resistor.

      The quoted 50–330 Ω range is a tutorial convenience, not a universal electrical rule:

      • 330 Ω makes sense for red. The estimate is about 3.9 mA, close to the RP2350 pad’s default 4 mA drive setting.
      • 50 Ω does not make sense for every LED. With the same red-LED estimate it asks for 26 mA. Do not use it as a general Pico recommendation.
      • Values above 330 Ω remain valid. They reduce current and brightness. Your 1 kΩ part is intentionally conservative.

      The correct method is: find Vf, choose a modest current, calculate R, then select the next higher standard value. Higher is the safer rounding direction.

      What “4 mA” and “12 mA” mean on Pico 2 The RP2350 pad has selectable 2, 4, 8, and 12 mA drive strengths; the register reset value is 4 mA. These settings describe output-drive behaviour, not permission to draw unlimited current up to 12 mA. For a beginner indicator, design around 2–4 mA and stay inside the chip’s 100 mA total IOVDD budget. See the RP2350 datasheet, GPIO pad register and electrical-characteristics sections.
      Rules of thumb worth memorising

      For this lesson: red LED + 1 kΩ. It uses parts you own and keeps current low.

      330 Ω is a useful future purchase for red indicator LEDs at 3.3 V.

      When in doubt, go higher. Too much resistance costs brightness. Too little can overload the LED or GPIO.

      The trap: blue and white LEDs on 3.3 V

      Blue and white LEDs often have Vf close to 3.3 V. Small changes in the particular LED or GPIO voltage then change the current greatly, so the simple fixed-Vf estimate becomes unreliable. Do not compensate by removing the resistor. Start with red; revisit other colours after identifying their exact parts or measuring them.
