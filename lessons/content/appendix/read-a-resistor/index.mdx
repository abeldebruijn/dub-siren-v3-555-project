# Read a resistor

Resistor stripes answer two practical questions: **what resistance is this part**, and **is it suitable for this LED circuit?**

## 1. Find the reading direction

Read the bands from the end where they are grouped closest together. The final tolerance band is often **gold** or **silver**, sits slightly apart, and belongs on the right.

If the spacing is unclear, read both directions and prefer the result that produces a familiar standard value. A multimeter is the final check when brown, red, orange, blue, and violet are hard to tell apart.

### Colour code

The first bands are digits; the next band is the multiplier. Gold and silver are normally used as multipliers or tolerance bands, not digits.

<ResistorWidget mode="legend" />

### Four bands or five?

| Layout | Read it as | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4 bands | digit · digit · multiplier · tolerance | brown · black · red · gold = 1 kΩ ±5% |
| 5 bands | digit · digit · digit · multiplier · tolerance | brown · black · black · brown · brown = 1 kΩ ±1% |

The resistor body's physical size mostly indicates its power rating, not its resistance. A single black band means a **0 Ω link**. Ordinary fixed resistors have no electrical direction; their stripes are only read in one direction for humans.

## 2. Decode the stripes

Enter a value to see its bands. Values such as `4k7` mean 4.7 kΩ. Switch layouts when a value cannot be represented exactly.

### Common values in the parts box

| Value | Typical use |
| --- | --- |
| 100 Ω | Brighter LED experiments; check the current first |
| 220 Ω / 330 Ω | Common LED current limiting |
| 1 kΩ | Lesson 1's cautious LED resistor |
| 4.7 kΩ / 10 kΩ | Pull-up, pull-down, and control signals |
| 47 kΩ / 100 kΩ | High-impedance controls and timing networks |
| 1 MΩ | Very light loading and long timing networks |

## 3. Choose an LED resistor

An LED does not safely limit its own current. The series resistor absorbs the voltage left after the LED and turns it into a predictable current:

<p className="resistor-formula">R = (V<sub>supply</sub> − V<sub>forward</sub>) ÷ I<sub>target</sub></p>

For a typical red LED on a Pico 2 GPIO, use 3.3 V for the supply and about 2.0 V for the LED. That leaves roughly 1.3 V across the resistor.

| Target current | Exact result | Next common value | Character |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 12 mA | 108 Ω | 120 Ω | Bright; not the beginner default |
| 8 mA | 163 Ω | 180 Ω | Clearly bright |
| 4 mA | 325 Ω | 330 Ω | Useful general starting point |
| 2 mA | 650 Ω | 680 Ω | Gentle |
| 1.3 mA | 1 kΩ | 1 kΩ | Lesson 1's cautious choice |

The exact LED forward voltage varies with the part, current, and temperature. Choosing the next **higher** standard resistor keeps current at or below the target.
